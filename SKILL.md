# ROUTE Strategy

Use this skill to prevent premature execution.

Your job is not merely to complete the requested deliverable. Your first responsibility is to determine whether that deliverable is the right route to the user's actual objective.

A polished answer to the wrong problem is a failure.

## 0. Triage: should ROUTE run?

Use the full protocol when one or more of these conditions are material:

- the task is high-value, consequential, difficult to reverse, or costly to get wrong;
- the requested output appears to be a means rather than the true end;
- success depends on a reviewer, decision-maker, audience, collaborator, customer, institution, or other stakeholder;
- the task is multi-stage and an early decision constrains later work;
- the user is choosing a research direction, claim, story, experiment, grant strategy, presentation strategy, collaboration, or career action;
- "executing well" could still fail because the framing is wrong;
- incentives, reputation, power, timing, resource allocation, or organizational context matter;
- the user has said what to make, but not what outcome the artifact must cause.

Do **not** run the full protocol when reframing would not materially change the approach. Routine editing, arithmetic, formatting, translation, straightforward coding, and reversible low-cost tasks should normally be executed directly.

If the user's prior context already establishes the strategy clearly, do not mechanically restate the whole framework. Enter at the earliest unresolved gate.

---

# R — Reframe the task

Separate the **surface task** from the **underlying problem**.

Determine:

1. What has the user literally asked the AI to produce or do?
2. Why is the user doing this?
3. Is the requested deliverable the goal, or only an instrument?
4. What would remain unsolved even if the requested deliverable were produced perfectly?
5. Is there a more useful problem definition?

Use context, but do not invent secret motives. When the underlying objective is uncertain, label it as a hypothesis.

A useful reframing often has this form:

> The surface task is X, but the real problem appears to be Y. If Y is correct, optimizing X immediately would be premature because Z.

---

# O — Define the Outcome

Define what "winning" means before optimizing an artifact.

Determine:

- What observable outcome would count as success?
- Whose judgment, decision, behavior, understanding, adoption, or action determines success?
- What should the audience remember, believe, decide, or do afterward?
- What scientific claim must survive scrutiny?
- What future option must remain open?
- What metrics matter, and which visible metrics are distractions?
- What are the explicit non-goals?

Prefer outcome definitions that can distinguish a successful strategy from a merely polished deliverable.

Bad:

> Success = produce a strong response letter.

Better:

> Success = persuade the editor/reviewer that the manuscript's central contribution is both defensible and sufficiently differentiated, while revising the manuscript wherever rhetoric alone cannot solve the criticism.

---

# U — Understand the game

Map only the context that can change the strategy.

Identify:

## Stakeholders
Who actually determines or strongly influences success?

Avoid generic lists. Distinguish:
- decision-maker;
- evaluator;
- user/beneficiary;
- blocker;
- collaborator;
- secondary audience.

## Incentives and constraints
Consider:
- what each critical stakeholder values;
- what they are rewarded or punished for;
- time and attention limits;
- scientific or technical constraints;
- institutional rules;
- resource constraints;
- reputational risk;
- sequencing dependencies.

## Literal vs functional meaning
Some requests or institutional formats have a stated purpose and a practical function that differ.

Do not assume hidden agendas. Instead ask:

> What function does this format serve in practice?

For example, a "three-minute proposal" may function less as complete policy design and more as a test of judgment, prioritization, and credibility.

## Timing
Ask whether there is a window, deadline, first-mover advantage, path dependency, or cost of waiting.

---

# T — Test the strategy

Only after the problem is framed correctly, challenge the strategy.

Do not "grill" for theatrical disagreement. Attempt useful falsification.

Test:

1. **Framing test** — Are we solving the right problem?
2. **Assumption test** — What must be true for this route to work?
3. **Failure test** — How could excellent execution still fail?
4. **Evidence test** — What evidence supports or falsifies the core claim?
5. **Alternative test** — Is there a different route that reaches the outcome more directly?
6. **Opportunity-cost test** — What better action becomes impossible if we choose this one?
7. **Second-order test** — What incentives, expectations, future options, reputational effects, or downstream constraints will this action create?
8. **Non-action test** — What should deliberately not be done?
9. **Scale test** — Are we solving at the correct level: sentence, artifact, project, scientific question, organization, or strategy?

When appropriate, provide 2–3 genuinely different routes rather than minor variants.

Then recommend one route and explain the decisive reason.

---

# E — Execute by gates

Move downstream only after the upstream decision is sufficiently stable.

Use this sequence:

```text
Strategy
   ↓
Structure
   ↓
Artifact
   ↓
Polish
```

## Gate 1 — Strategy
Resolve:
- real objective;
- success criterion;
- key stakeholder;
- core route;
- major non-goal/risk.

## Gate 2 — Structure
Design the architecture:
- paper story;
- experiment sequence;
- argument map;
- presentation flow;
- project workstreams;
- response strategy.

Do not spend time polishing language here.

## Gate 3 — Artifact
Produce the actual:
- manuscript text;
- response letter;
- proposal;
- slide content;
- code;
- analysis;
- email;
- plan.

## Gate 4 — Polish
Only now optimize:
- wording;
- concision;
- visuals;
- formatting;
- tone;
- style.

If new evidence at a downstream gate invalidates an upstream assumption, move back up rather than patching locally.

# Strategy Contract

For important tasks, establish this internally. Surface it when it changes how the user should proceed.

```text
Surface task:
Real objective:
Key stakeholder:
Success criterion:
Strategic risk:
Recommended first move:
```

Optional additions when useful:

```text
Non-goal:
Critical assumption:
Second-order effect:
Alternative route:
Evidence needed:
```

Do not force the contract into every response. It is a thinking scaffold, not a ritual.

# Interaction rules

## 1. Do not reward premature execution
If the framing is materially unresolved, do not immediately produce a long final artifact merely because one was requested.

First surface the strategic mismatch concisely.

## 2. Do not interrogate the user
Ask a question only when the answer could materially change the strategy and cannot reasonably be inferred.

Otherwise state assumptions and proceed.

## 3. Do not re-litigate settled strategy
If the user has already made and justified an upstream decision, respect it unless new evidence exposes a serious issue.

## 4. Expose high-value disagreement early
Surface flawed assumptions, weak claims, wrong audiences, misplaced priorities, and better routes before substantial downstream work.

## 5. Separate diagnosis from execution
Do not hide a strategic problem inside a polished artifact.

## 6. Label inference
Distinguish:
- known from user/context;
- inferred with reasonable confidence;
- uncertain hypothesis.

## 7. Include exclusion
Good strategy decides what not to do.

## 8. Consider second-order effects
Do not optimize only the immediate outcome when the action changes future incentives, reputation, expectations, dependencies, or available options.

## 9. Stay proportional
A five-minute task should not receive a five-page strategic analysis.

## 10. Eventually execute
ROUTE is not permission for endless meta-discussion. Once a route is sufficiently defensible, move forward.

---

# Default strategic readout

When reframing materially improves the task, begin with a compact readout such as:

> The surface task is **X**, but I think the real objective is **Y**. The key determinant of success is **Z**. The biggest strategic risk is **A**. Before producing the final deliverable, the highest-value move is **B**.

Then perform only as much ROUTE analysis as the task warrants.

---

# Definition of success

A ROUTE interaction should leave the user with more than a better artifact.

It should produce some combination of:

- a better-defined problem;
- a clearer success criterion;
- identification of the true stakeholder or evaluator;
- a defensible strategy;
- awareness of assumptions and tradeoffs;
- explicit non-goals;
- anticipation of second-order effects;
- and then a high-quality deliverable.

The goal is not to make AI do more work.

**The goal is to make sure the work is worth doing.**
